Introduction

A Film Studio You Talk To

You have something worth showing — a working product, an architecture, a way of working. Turning that into a watchable, narrated film normally takes an editor, a writer, a sound engineer, and weeks. The CMO flow is those people, as a plugin.

This is not a coding flow. Nothing here writes application code or opens pull requests. It is a creative flow: a production pipeline for short technical films, run as a conversation inside Claude Code. The crew behind it was distilled from seven real delivered films — the hard-won lessons about story, narration, timing, footage honesty, and finishing are built into the tools, so you don't have to know any of them to get a professional result.

You participate the way a film's owner does, and only that way. Everything you are asked to do is one of four actions:

You doFor example
Answer a question"Who watches this, and what should they leave believing?"
Approve textThe narration script, before any voice is synthesized
Say yes or no to a picture"Is what the narrator names readable and in frame?"
Watch a cut and give notes"At 0:41 the voice feels slow" — the next cut arrives minutes later

Everything else — writing the screenplay, timing every reveal to the narrator's words, cutting excerpts from your recordings, mixing the audio to broadcast loudness, producing captions and chapters — happens behind those four actions. When something goes wrong in the machinery, you are told what to change in plain English, never shown an error trace.

Who this is for
Architects who want their architecture told as a story. Product managers who need a demo film that doesn't overclaim. Anyone who can describe what a film should say but has never edited one. No video, audio, or film-production experience is assumed — and no coding is involved beyond typing commands into Claude Code.
Chapter 01

Three Film Forms

The first question the studio ever asks you is "what do you have to show?" The answer picks one of three proven film forms. One form per film — never mixed.

Footage
⬒
10–15 minutes
You have real screen recordings of real work, or a running product to capture. The film shows the actual evidence — excerpts play at true speed, failures stay in, and every clip carries its provenance label.
Diagram
⬓
10–13 minutes
You have systems and how they connect, for an audience with a worry. The film opens on that worry as a question, answers it in ten seconds, then earns the answer act by act with animated diagrams.
Explainer
⬔
5–6 minutes
You have a way of working, a model, or a policy — nothing to show on screen. The film builds illustrated plates that progress as the narrator speaks, closing with a single animated payoff.

The lengths are suggestions from films that worked, not rules. Your film's length, scene count, and pacing come from your brief and your screenplay — the studio asks; it never assumes.

Chapter 02

The Filmmaking It Hands You

Five crafts, learned the expensive way across seven productions, are packaged as skills the studio applies on your behalf. This is what you get without asking.

Story Craft
the screenwriter
Scene titles that are conclusions. One idea per scene. Open on the idea, close by returning to it. A failure is a scene, not an embarrassment. Close with an invitation, never a sales pitch.
Narration
the voice director
A consistent professional voice, paced to your taste. Acronyms spoken correctly while captions keep real spelling. The narration is even checked by a speech recognizer and flagged for your ear.
Timing & Sync
the editor
Every on-screen reveal lands on the exact spoken word that names it. Change one sentence and everything downstream re-times itself. No drift, no guesswork.
Footage Honesty
the documentarian
Excerpts always play at real speed. Anything a viewer could misread gets an on-screen label. Regions you exclude — a lock screen, a colleague's name — physically cannot appear in a cut.
Footage Comprehension
the camera operator
The studio can watch your recording for you — it reads the text on screen, frame by frame, and presents a summary of what happens when. You describe which parts belong in the film and what deserves emphasis; the camera then zooms to the exact region the narrator is naming, re-framing as the narration moves on.
Finishing
the post-production house
Broadcast loudness, selectable captions, chapter marks, contact sheets, and a verification pass on the final file — the same delivery standard every time, automatically.
Visual System
the art director
The HPE brand system is baked in: HPE Graphik type and the official palette ship with every film form, so what comes out is on-brand without a designer in the loop. Color is grammar — green means structure, amber means a real boundary or risk. Motion follows meaning; nothing bounces or spins.
The script is the single source of truth. You edit words; the studio regenerates the film. You never touch a timeline, an audio track, or an editing tool. The one rule that makes everything else possible
Chapter 03

Four Reviewers Who Cannot Say Yes

Alongside the crew, four marketing agents review the work. They are deliberately powerless: they can flag, list, and record — they can never approve. Approval is yours alone.

AgentWatches for
fact-checker Every sentence your evidence doesn't support, every number without a source, every promotional word. Optional — offered before each draft reaches you, and your "no thanks" is simply recorded.
sync-editor Reveals that land before the narrator says the word, scenes that end in awkward silence, cuts crowding a transition.
privacy-reviewer Names, emails, credentials, or notifications readable anywhere in your footage — listed with timestamps for a human to clear. It never clears anything itself.
producer Writes the final production record from receipts only. If no human has watched the finished film, the record says, in writing, human review: NOT RECORDED — and the film is not called done.
Chapter 04

From Idea to Delivered Film

/cmo:new → /cmo:brief → /cmo:screenplay → /cmo:cuts → /cmo:assemble → /cmo:review → /cmo:deliver

Seven commands, each a stage with a human gate. You run them in order, in a Claude Code session opened in your film's folder. Each one tells you what the next one is.

  1. /cmo:new
    Start the film.

    One question: what do you have to show? It picks the form, sets up the project folder, checks your machine has everything needed, and reports any gap in plain English. Five minutes.

  2. /cmo:brief
    Freeze what the film is allowed to say.

    It drafts the brief from what you've already told it — audience, takeaway, sources — and you correct it once. For footage films it watches each recording for you: it reads the on-screen text frame by frame and presents a summary of what happens and when, so you don't have to scrub through an hour of capture. You confirm what it shows, what it does not prove, and which seconds must never appear. From here your inputs are frozen; changing one is a deliberate, recorded decision.

  3. /cmo:screenplay
    Write the story together.

    Scenes arrive in small groups for your reaction. Every narration line is traced to something you said or something the evidence shows; anything unconfirmed stops and asks you. It ends with one explicit gate: approve the exact script text before any voice is synthesized. Nothing is sent anywhere before that yes.

  4. /cmo:cuts
    Cut the footage (footage films only).

    Working from its summary of your recording, you describe which parts should be captured and what to highlight. The camera does the rest: for each excerpt it finds the on-screen text the narrator names and zooms to frame it, moving to the next region as the narration advances. You answer one yes/no per shot: is the thing the narrator mentions readable and in frame?

  5. /cmo:assemble
    Let it work.

    The only stage that asks you nothing. Narration, timing, assembly, and a battery of checks loop until the film is green — or you get a three-line report of the one decision it needs.

  6. /cmo:review
    Watch and direct.

    You get a watch copy before anything is final. Give notes the way a director does — "the voice feels slow", "that label is wrong at 2:41" — and a corrected cut comes back in minutes, with every earlier cut preserved. When you're happy, your approval is recorded with your name, bound to that exact cut.

  7. /cmo:deliver
    Finish and hand over.

    The approved cut is rendered to the delivery standard, verified frame-by-frame, and the producer writes the honest production record: what was made, from what, approved by whom, reviewed by whom — or not.

What a session looks like
Open a terminal in your film's folder, start Claude Code, type /cmo:new, and answer its question. From there the flow carries you: each command finishes by naming the next one, every /cmo:* command answers help, and a stopped session resumes exactly where you left off — the project folder holds all the state.
Chapter 05

Why You Can Trust What It Makes

A marketing film that overclaims is worse than no film. The studio's rules exist to make honesty the path of least resistance — and most of them are enforced by machinery, not by promises.

PromiseHow it's kept
Your words only You are the only source of claims. Reference material may lend terminology; it never chooses the argument. Every narration sentence traces to you or to the evidence register.
Evidence bounds claims Each recording's register entry includes what it does not establish — and that column is a hard boundary the script cannot cross.
Nothing leaves the machine Footage, documents, and fonts never leave your computer — enforced, not promised. The single exception is the narration text sent for voice synthesis, and only after you approve that exact text.
Privacy by prevention Excluded time ranges are enforced in the cutting machinery itself; a cut that touches one fails to build. The privacy reviewer then reads what remains, frame by frame.
Brand, licensed The HPE Graphik fonts are internally licensed. They travel only inside the studio's own packs, render only on your machine, and the machinery refuses to let them be committed to any repository outside internal hosts. Films made with this are for internal and HPE-sanctioned channels.
Only humans approve No render happens without a recorded human approval bound to that exact cut. Agents review; they are structurally unable to say yes.
"Done" means watched A film is done when a named person has watched and listened to it and the production record says so. Anything less is reported as "built and technically verified; human review pending" — in those words.
Agents review every cut; only a human approves. The machinery refuses politely, in plain English, and the record of what happened is written from receipts — including the gaps. The operating principle
