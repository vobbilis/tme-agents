Field Notes · 01

Story & Claims

Every rejected cut in seven productions was a story or claims problem. Not one was a technical failure.

01

The first evidence film opened on a ticket. The review called it a challenge-response pitch, and the cut was redone from the top. Films open on the idea, and close by returning to it.

02

A seven-minute engineering film led with repository statistics — commit counts, contributor names, test totals. The owner rejected the entire film. The approved version led with how the organization works, and let working screens show the activity instead of asserting it. Lead with the change, never the audit.

03

One production ended with the push blocked by access controls. The film kept it: "Local commit retained. Push access blocked." Those frames turned out to be the most persuasive in the film, because a slide cannot fake them. The real outcome stays in, especially when it is a failure.

04

An early cut hedged every sentence — "the page describes the standard, not proof that…" — until the hedging was the loudest thing in the film. A limitation is said once, where it changes the next decision, and not again.

05

A fifteen-minute film about engineering productivity reports zero productivity numbers, and says why in one sentence: there was no comparable baseline to measure against. A number needs a source, an interval, a definition and a denominator — or it is not said.

06

The owner confirmed three engineers; the footage showed three recordings. The narration says "engineer three takes up the implementation" — and never implies the handover itself was filmed, because it wasn't. People are not agents, and recordings are not handovers.

07

A film was briefed from a voice memo. The transcript heard "glass wing" where the owner had said something else entirely, so the film said "shared security harness" until the owner confirmed the real name. Unconfirmed names stay generic. The owner's words are the only claims source.

Field Notes · 02

Voice & Timing

The narrator's word timings are the film's clock. Everything that drifted, drifted because something ignored that.

08

The narrator pronounced "SecOps" as "Secretary Ops". No one caught it by reading — it was caught by running speech recognition on the actual audio and comparing what was heard against what was written. Pronunciation is verified by listening, never by trusting the text.

09

A seven-minute film drifted eight seconds between picture and voice by the end, because wall-clock pacing was trusted. The fix measured the actual recording of every scene and corrected each one individually. Measured speech sets durations. Estimates don't.

10

Every on-screen reveal is tied to a three-to-six-word phrase from that scene's narration, and the build fails if the phrase appears twice or not at all. Change one sentence and everything downstream re-times itself. Reveals land on spoken words, not on guessed seconds.

Field Notes · 03

Footage

Footage is the one thing a slide cannot fake — which is exactly why nothing about it may be faked.

11

Two cuts used highlight rectangles that followed the on-screen text, with camera pans between them. The review called them distracting. The third cut shipped with zero rectangles and one fixed, readable framing per excerpt. The camera frames once and holds. Emphasis comes from the narration, not from motion.

12

No excerpt has ever been sped up, slowed down, or looped — even when real work meant watching a progress bar. Footage plays at real speed. If it is too long, cut it shorter; don't make it lie.

13

A held frame looks identical to playing footage. A sped-up excerpt looks like a fast product. Viewers can't tell the difference, so the films tell them: HELD FRAME, EDITED EXCERPTS, RECORDING PENDING — in amber, on screen. Anything a viewer could misread gets a label.

14

One source recording contained a lock screen at minute twenty; a later one caught a window with colleagues' names. Both became exclusion ranges written into the cutting machinery itself — a cut that touches one fails to build, rather than depending on an editor's memory. Privacy boundaries are code, not notes.

15

A capture session once exposed a real product bug mid-recording. The film did not fix it, hide it, or fake around it: the bug was written up, and the capture took a different legitimate path through the product. A bug found on camera is documented and routed around — never repaired for the film.

16

The automated product-capture adapter refuses to film anything except instances running fabricated demo data — the check is in the code, not in a guideline. Real day-to-day usage is recorded by its owner, and reviewed for privacy frame by frame. Live systems with real data are never filmed by automation.

Field Notes · 04

Finishing & Honesty

The last mile is where polished tools quietly overclaim. These rules keep the record straight.

17

Every film's verification report ends the same way: the checks passed, and a complete watch-and-listen by a named human is still required. The production record will print "human review: NOT RECORDED" — in those words — rather than round up to done. Done means a named person watched and listened. Machines never approve a film.

18

An owner once said "finish without further approval stops," and the production honored it — by recording those exact words, with a timestamp, in the approval itself, where the final record prints them. Authority can skip a gate. The skip is quoted verbatim, forever.

Field Notes · 05

Still Learning

Some of these notes are from this month, learned while building the studio itself. The method doesn't retire.

+1

The studio's first reference film was commissioned at exactly fifteen minutes — and that shape quietly fossilized into the tooling as hardcoded constants. The owner caught it with one question: "shouldn't this come from the interview?" Lengths, scene counts and act structure now come only from the brief and the screenplay. A constant inherited from a reference film is a bug.

+2

For weeks the project repeated that a preview tool's audio "was unreliable on two runs". The owner corrected the record: the audio was fine — the operator hadn't learned the playback controls. Every document that carried the claim was fixed the same day. The claims discipline applies to our own documentation.

+3

The first film made by the studio itself got a one-line review from its owner: "the voice somehow feels slow." The note became a recorded disposition, the pace changed, and a corrected cut existed minutes later — with the first cut preserved untouched. A review note is a change of the words, and everything else regenerates.
